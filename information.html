INFORMATION LITERACY

Don't just ask, is it true?

Ask who published it, when it was published, what evidence supports it, whether other reliable sources agree, and whether the wording is designed to provoke a reaction.

Fact or Fake?

Evaluate fictional claims and learn to think critically about the information you encounter.

CASE 01

Scientists say this one trick fixes every problem.

No named source, no date, and no evidence are provided.

Choose the safer approach.
CASE 02

Official report: New study confirms health benefits of a specific fruit.

The report cites a named university, includes the publication date, and links to peer-reviewed research.

Choose the safer approach.

Evaluation checklist

Use these questions whenever you encounter information online.

Check the source

Who published it? Is the author or organisation identifiable and credible? Look for an About page or editorial policy.

Check the date

Information can become outdated. Check when it was published or last updated, especially for health and science topics.

Check the author

Does the author have relevant expertise? Are their credentials verifiable? Anonymous content requires extra scrutiny.

Look for evidence

Does the content cite data, studies, or verifiable facts? Claims without evidence should be treated cautiously.

Compare sources

Check whether multiple reliable sources agree. If only one source reports a claim, verify before accepting it.

Watch for manipulation

Emotionally charged language, ALL CAPS, and excessive exclamation marks may signal attempts to provoke rather than inform.
